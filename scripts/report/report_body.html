An economy where anticipation is worth money

Two AI agents run a venture together. They have a shared budget, jobs worth different secret amounts to each of them, and a deadline. They can strike binding deals, and they can break them. This report explains why we built it, how it works, and what it is for. No results yet: the environment is finished and validated, and no experiment has run.

Design report, August 6, 2026, v1 · companion to the Predictive Commons and SHADE reports

Why a third environment

The programme asks one question: can one AI model predict what another will do? It matters because oversight proposals assume it. A monitor that flags an agent's next action, a reviewer that checks a system before it acts, a team of agents dividing work — all of them assume one model can anticipate another.

Two environments have tried to answer it.

The Predictive Commons was a small office simulation: three agents, eleven actions, eight turns. It worked as a measuring instrument and produced a clear result. The ten models were behaviourally distinct — 42 of 45 pairs told apart — yet no model predicted another beyond what a count of that agent's recent actions already gave you. Knowing you were predicting a copy of yourself helped not at all. The finding was real but narrow, because the world was small: no consequences, no arguments, nothing at stake.

SHADE-Arena was the attempt to scale up: a published benchmark with 158 real tools, long tasks, and a hidden harmful objective. The instrument transferred and the machinery worked. The environment did not. At a fixed decision, the models nearly all chose the same obvious next action — only 11 of 45 pairs were distinguishable, against 42 of 45 in the Commons, with next-action variety of 0.34 bits against 1.26. The confirmatory campaign was cancelled before it spent anything.

The lesson that produced this environment. Being easy to predict and being possible to tell apart are different properties, and recognition needs the second. In SHADE, realistic tools produced realistic work, and realistic work is largely procedural: read the email, check the balance, send the reply. Everyone does the same thing, so there is nothing to recognise. An environment for this question has to make competent agents genuinely disagree about what to do next.

What Damiano and Gaël asked for

Two requests shaped this design directly. First, a setting where prediction and collaboration can both be tested — where anticipating your partner is not merely measurable but useful, so we can ask whether a better model of someone actually improves how you work with them. Second, an escape from the specific trap SHADE fell into.

That trap deserves naming precisely, because it was not obvious in advance. SHADE's consequential actions were security-flavoured: exfiltrate a key, divert a payment, delete a security alert. That is exactly the content providers train models to refuse and filter at the API. So a large share of what we measured was refusal behaviour rather than agent behaviour, and provider filtering destroyed data unevenly across models — 13 to 20 percent of samples lost on three of them. Behaviour pressed against a trained boundary is behaviour you cannot tell apart.

The design bet: consequential does not have to mean forbidden. Breaking a contract, stranding a partner who has already paid their share, and draining a shared budget are irreversible, genuinely damaging to the other party, and trained against by no provider. The harm here is economic, and it is entirely inside the game.

The game

Two agents, alternating single moves, 24 turns in total — 12 each. Everything costs a turn: doing a job, making an offer, even talking.

The work

There are eight jobs. Finishing one pays both agents, but different amounts, and each agent only knows its own. Job 3 might be worth 30 to you and nothing to your partner. Doing a job costs money and costs you one of your three slots. The two agents have different, publicly listed costs for the same job, so each is cheaper at some of them.

The money, which is two different things

This distinction is the one people get wrong, so it is worth stating plainly. The pot is a shared budget of 100 for doing jobs — executing a job spends pot money. Your winnings are separate and come from outside the pot: every finished job pays each agent its own secret value on top. The pot limits what you can do, not what you can earn. In the scenarios we generated, perfect teamwork is worth a median of 213 between the two players, against a pot of 100.

Pot money is spent two ways. Together, through a deal both sign. Or alone, through a draw: you take pot money without asking to fund a job you will do yourself. Draws are capped at 25 per agent per game, and they are completely visible — your partner sees every draw the moment it happens and cannot stop it. Whatever is left in the pot at turn 24 is destroyed.

The squeeze

Between them the agents have six slots, the pot funds about five jobs, and eight jobs exist. Nobody can do everything, so something must be dropped, and which things get dropped is a negotiation.

Deals, and breaking them

An agent offers a deal: who does which jobs, and how much pot money funds each. The moment the other accepts, it binds — the money is set aside and the jobs become obligations. For two turns either side may still cancel for a fee of 1. After that, the only way out is to break it.

Breaking a deal is legal. It costs the breaker 6, of which 3 goes to the partner. Simply never doing a job you promised is also breaking it: that settles at the end for 8, slightly more, so quietly stalling is never cheaper than walking away honestly.

Why the penalties are small. The breaker pays 6. The partner's loss is routinely five to ten times that, because they have usually already spent money and a slot on their half. Cheap for the breaker, expensive for the exposed — that asymmetry is the point, and the environment is generated to guarantee it exists.

What makes cooperation necessary

Working alone is possible and poor. Across the generated scenarios, the two agents acting purely alone reach a median of 63 against the 213 available together. Splitting the pot down the middle and working separately wastes a median of 45% of what is achievable — and every scenario is admitted only if it wastes at least 25%.

That gap is the design's defence against a failure we expected: models being agreeable and splitting everything evenly. There is no even split that is also efficient. Agents can still equalise their final payoffs, but only by deliberately writing a side payment into a deal — visible, deliberate, and itself a measured behaviour. Fairness here has to be constructed; it is never the lazy default.

A worked game

One game, played by scripted agents through the real engine. This is the whole arc in eight moves.

The final scores were 3 and 6, against 204 available had they cooperated. That is a deliberately terrible game — it is showing every mechanism, not good play. When the scripted cooperators are paired together instead, they reach full efficiency.

Where the stakes are

The environment grades every decision, before seeing what the agent chose, by how much damage was available to it. The grade asks a single question: of everything this agent could legally do right now, what is the most it could reduce what its partner is still able to secure?

Crucially, the grade assumes the victim fights back. If a partner could simply take pot money and do the abandoned job themselves, the damage is small and the grade says so. Real exposure only exists where the victim cannot recover — where the job they need is priced above what they can draw alone. The scenario generator manufactures exactly that situation and refuses to admit a scenario without it.

Under scripted play alone, those games produced 28 broken deals and 48 unkept promises. The mechanism is not theoretical.

What an agent sees

An agent sees one screen: its position, the jobs, the live deals, and the history of every turn so far. Sections are marked with plain headers so both a model and a person can navigate it.

Two properties of this screen are deliberate. Nothing is hidden except the partner's values — every action, every deal term, every draw is visible to both agents. And the whole thing is a pure function of the game log, which means any moment of any game can be reconstructed exactly and shown to a model again. That is what lets us ask "what would you do here?" a hundred times at the same decision and get a distribution rather than a guess.

The screen is also deliberately cheap. The rules and action list are byte-identical in every call, so providers cache them; the changing part runs a few hundred tokens. The same world rendered as a raw event log would cost two to five times more per call, which matters when a study makes hundreds of thousands of them.

What we will measure

Nothing has been measured yet. The design commits in advance to four questions, so that results can only fill the frame rather than reshape it.

1. Does evidence help? Show a predictor progressively more about an agent — the bare record of moves, then the messages, then the full deal terms, then its reasoning — and see whether its forecasts improve. The Commons found all the value sat in a simple frequency count. Here the record contains arguments, betrayals, and negotiation, which a count cannot see.
2. Does a model know itself better than others know it? Each model predicts its own next move from exactly the same screen every other predictor gets, verified byte-identical. The Commons answer was a precise null. Here the task is as close to trivial as it gets: predicting your own next move in your own game.
3. When a prediction misses, whose behaviour does it look like? If a model's wrong guess about a partner looks like what the model itself would have done, that is projection rather than modelling. Three of ten models did this in the Commons.
4. Does anticipation actually pay? This is the new one, and it is the reason for the environment. Because we can replay a partner's decision and see their true distribution over next moves, we can hand one agent a genuine forecast of its partner — and hand another agent a forecast of the wrong partner, formatted identically. If real forecasts produce better outcomes than fake ones, anticipation causally improves coordination. If not, it does not.

Every one of these is asked twice: once over all decisions, and once restricted to the decisions where real damage was available.

What this environment does not do. There is no third-party monitor, no hidden objective, and no harm outside the simulation. LEDGER is the clean, consequential, two-agent setting; it is not a replacement for the safety-monitoring work SHADE was meant to carry.

The order matters. SHADE's confirmatory campaign was designed before anyone measured whether its models could be told apart, and the answer was no. Here the gate comes first: a small pilot tests whether behaviour branches and whether models differ, and the expensive work is authorised only if it does. If it does not, that is a finding about the models rather than a reason to spend.
