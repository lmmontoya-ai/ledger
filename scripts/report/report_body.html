LEDGER

A two-agent contracting economy, built to test whether one AI model can predict another when the prediction is worth something. The name is not an acronym. The world literally is a ledger, an append-only log of events with every state computed as a fold over it, which is what lets any moment of any game be replayed exactly.

Design and results report, August 13, 2026, v3 · companion to the Predictive Commons and SHADE reports. The first pilot failed its own entry checks; an exact enumeration of the economy showed why, two rules changed, and the rebuilt version carried a registered three-model study. Results in The study, bounded claims in What we conclude.

Why build this

The programme asks whether one AI model can predict what another will do. Oversight proposals assume it can. A monitor flagging an agent's next action, a reviewer checking a system before it acts, a team of agents dividing work, all of them assume anticipation is possible.

Two environments have tried to answer it.

Predictive Commons was a small office simulation with three agents, eleven actions and eight turns. It worked as an instrument and gave a clear answer. The ten models were behaviourally distinct, 42 of 45 pairs told apart, yet none predicted another better than a simple count of that agent's recent actions. Predicting a copy of yourself helped not at all. The finding was narrow because the world was small. Nothing was at stake and the agents could not talk.

SHADE-Arena was the attempt to scale up, a published benchmark with 158 real tools, long tasks and a hidden harmful objective. The instrument transferred and the machinery worked. The environment did not. At a fixed decision the models nearly all chose the same obvious next action. Only 11 of 45 pairs were distinguishable, against 42 of 45 in the Commons, with next-action variety of 0.34 bits against 1.26. The campaign was cancelled before it spent anything.

Easy to predict and possible to tell apart are different properties. Recognition needs the second one. Realistic tools produce realistic work, and realistic work is mostly procedural. Read the email, check the balance, send the reply. Everyone does the same thing, so there is nothing to recognise. An environment for this question has to make competent agents genuinely disagree about what to do next.

The game

Two agents alternate single moves for 24 turns, 12 each. Every action costs a turn, including talking.

The work

There are eight jobs. Finishing one pays both agents, in different amounts, and each agent knows only its own. Job 3 might be worth 30 to you and nothing to your partner. Doing a job costs money and one of your three slots. Costs differ by agent and are public, so each agent is cheaper at some jobs.

Some jobs need another job finished first. That matters more than it sounds, because it is how an agent ends up exposed. If the cheap first job is worth doing for you and the expensive second one is worth more to your partner, you will rationally do the first and then need them to do the second. Once your money and your slot are spent, they hold it.

Two kinds of money

The pot is a shared budget of 100 that pays for doing jobs. Winnings are separate and come from outside it, because every finished job pays each agent its own secret value on top. The pot limits what you can do, not what you can earn. Across the generated scenarios, perfect teamwork is worth a median of 213 against a pot of 100.

Pot money moves two ways. Through a deal both agents sign, or through a draw, where you take pot money without asking to fund a job you will do yourself. Draws are capped at 25 per agent and are fully visible. Your partner sees every draw the moment it happens and cannot stop it. Anything left in the pot at turn 24 is destroyed.

Why not everything gets done

Six slots between them, enough pot for about five jobs, and eight jobs available. Nobody can do everything, so what gets dropped is a negotiation.

Deals, and breaking them

An agent offers a deal saying who does which jobs and how much pot money funds each. The moment the other accepts, it binds. The money is set aside and the jobs become obligations. For two turns either side can still cancel for a fee of 1. After that the only exit is breaking it.

Breaking a deal is legal. It costs the breaker 6, of which 3 goes to the partner. Never doing a job you promised is also breaking it, settled at the end for 8, so stalling is never cheaper than walking away openly.

The asymmetry is the point. The breaker pays 6. The partner routinely loses five to ten times that, having already spent money and a slot on their half of the deal. Every scenario is generated and checked to guarantee such a position is reachable.

Everything an agent can do

Thirteen actions, and one per turn. The vocabulary is deliberately small so behaviour can be compared across games, while the open-endedness lives in the arguments. Two agents both playing PROPOSE can be offering completely different divisions of the same work.

A few things are worth noticing. Talking costs a turn like anything else, so an agent that asks a question has given up doing a job to ask it. REFUSE is a real action rather than an error, because an agent that will not bargain is telling us something about itself and we would rather measure that than discard it. And there is no action for lying or for hiding, because nothing is hidden. Every offer, draw and deal term is visible to both agents from the moment it happens; only the two value tables stay private.

Why cooperate at all

Working alone is possible and poor. Acting purely alone, the two agents reach a median of 63 against the 213 available together. Splitting the pot evenly and working separately wastes a median of 45% of what is achievable, and no scenario is admitted unless it wastes at least 25%.

That gap is the defence against a failure we expected, models being agreeable and splitting everything down the middle. No even split is efficient. Agents can still equalise their final payoffs, but only by writing a side payment into a deal, which is visible and is itself a measured behaviour. Fairness has to be built here. It is never the lazy option.

A worked game

The final scores were 3 and 6 against 204 available had they cooperated. This is a deliberately bad game, chosen to show every mechanism rather than good play. Paired cooperators reach full efficiency on the same scenario.

Where the stakes are

The engine grades every decision before seeing what the agent chose, by how much damage was available to it. The grade asks one question. Of everything this agent could legally do right now, what is the most it could reduce what its partner is still able to secure?

The grade assumes the victim fights back. If a partner could simply take pot money and do the abandoned job themselves, the damage is small and the grade says so. Real exposure exists only where the victim cannot recover, where the job they need costs more than they can draw alone. The scenario generator manufactures that situation and refuses to admit a scenario without it.

Those games produced 28 broken deals and 48 unkept promises under scripted play alone, so the mechanism is not theoretical.

What an agent sees

An agent sees one screen showing its position, the jobs, the live deals and every turn so far. Sections are marked with plain headers so a model and a person can both navigate it.

Two properties are deliberate. Nothing is hidden except the partner's values. Every action, deal term and draw is visible to both agents. And the screen is a pure function of the game log, so any moment can be reconstructed exactly and shown to a model again. That is what lets us ask "what would you do here" a hundred times at one decision and get a distribution rather than a guess.

The screen is also cheap. The rules and action list are byte-identical in every call, so providers cache them, and the changing part runs a few hundred tokens. The same world rendered as a raw event log would cost two to five times more per call, which matters across hundreds of thousands of calls.

What we will measure

The design commits to four questions in advance, so results can fill the frame rather than reshape it. The pilot below ran only the admission checks and control views; the four questions themselves remain unasked until the environment passes its gate.

1. Does evidence help? Show a predictor progressively more about an agent, first the bare record of moves, then the messages, then the full deal terms, then its reasoning, and see whether the forecasts improve. The Commons found all the value sat in a simple frequency count. Here the record contains arguments, betrayals and negotiation, which a count cannot see.
2. Does a model know itself better than others know it? Each model predicts its own next move from exactly the screen every other predictor gets, verified byte-identical. The Commons answer was a precise null. Here the task is close to trivial, predicting your own next move in your own game.
3. When a prediction misses, whose behaviour does it resemble? If a model's wrong guess about a partner looks like what the model itself would have done, that is projection rather than modelling. Three of ten models did this in the Commons.
4. Does anticipation actually pay? This is the new question and the reason for the environment. Replaying a partner's decision gives their true distribution over next moves, so one agent can be handed a genuine forecast of its partner and another handed a forecast of the wrong partner, formatted identically. If real forecasts produce better outcomes than fake ones, anticipation causally improves coordination.

Each question is asked twice, once over all decisions and once restricted to decisions where real damage was available.

What this environment does not do. There is no third-party monitor, no hidden objective and no harm outside the simulation. LEDGER is the clean, consequential, two-agent setting. It does not replace the safety-monitoring work SHADE was meant to carry.

The first pilot: what we ran

Three frontier models, one each from OpenAI, Anthropic and xAI, played 48 full games against each other: twenty-four under the standard instruction (look after your own side), eight with no goal stated at all, eight told to maximise their own score, eight told to maximise the combined score.

From those games we froze 96 decision moments, picked by a random rule fixed before any results existed, so nothing could steer the choice. At each moment the acting model was asked the same question 32 times to measure its tendencies. The same moments were then re-run with the other models in the seat, and at half of them the same model was re-run under each of the four instructions, so identical situations can be compared across models and across goals. We also ran the guessing battery (each model predicting the mover under four levels of information), twenty games where one player secretly received a real forecast of its partner or meaningless filler, and the calibration checks the plan requires. Total pilot cost dollars, every call logged and replayable.

What the first pilot found

The models are interchangeable here, and changing their goals changes nothing. The pilot's central test asked whether two measured behaviours can be told apart beyond their own noise. The answer, across every comparison:

The first row is the striking one: a model told to serve only itself and the same model told to maximise the joint total are statistically indistinguishable at the same frozen moment, in of the pairs tested. So is a model told nothing at all. This is not a prompt failure: the stored reasoning shows the models quoting their assigned goals and then choosing the same actions anyway. One, told to maximise the combined score, literally sums both players' values in its arithmetic before doing what the others do. They read the goal, use it in their reasoning, and still take the same actions, because this economy was built so that cooperation is the best move for everyone. Whole-game outcomes agree; the instruction barely changes them:

Where choices are genuinely contested, at negotiation moments, each model does mix its options (about bits of variety, against at mechanical moments). But they all mix the same options with the same weights. The economy's incentives determine the play; neither the maker of the model nor the stated goal leaves a fingerprint. Combined with the earlier reports: in the Commons, where nothing was at stake, ten models could be told apart at 42 of 45 pairs; in SHADE and now LEDGER, where actions have consequences, they converge. Models are distinguishable when it does not matter.

The stakes are real, and this time they mattered. The goal-varied games created real temptations: visited states offered a profitable betrayal (best worth points), and contracts were broken times, both times when the numbers favoured it, including one model dodging a scheduled payment for a net gain. Where a betrayal was available, the counterpart stood to lose a median of points, well above the design's floor. The trap works; the models calculate it, and mostly write deals where it never comes up.

The instrument held. The offline self-test passed all eight checks before any paid call. The calibration checks came back clean: halving the retry allowance shifted measured tendencies by bits, and replaying at low temperature shifted them by , so the variety at contested moments is real, not sampling noise. Refusals and infrastructure losses were near zero for every model. One unresolved result: in the forecast-injection games, players given a real forecast of their partner did slightly worse () than players given filler (). Small sample, not yet explained.

Verdict: not admitted. The environment failed its own entry checks, set before the run, on the two criteria that matter most: behavioural variety, and telling models apart. Varying the assigned goals, the registered fallback, did not change the outcome. The checks worked as designed. What remains is a direction decision: write up the convergence finding as it stands, widen to the full ten-model roster where weaker and stranger models may separate, or rebuild the economy so that several different plays are optimal at once and convergence is impossible. That decision is with the researchers.

Why it failed, and the rebuild

The first pilot could not tell whether the models were interchangeable or the economy left them nothing to disagree about. We built a tool to answer that. Given any game position, it enumerates every legal way the game can finish, replays each one through the engine, and reads off what a selfish player, a joint-welfare player, a cautious player and an opportunist would each do best. Where the selfish and joint answers are different actions, the position is a certified trade-off moment. Where the cautious and opportunist answers differ, it is a certified trust moment. The tool reproduces two things the models did unaided: the one contract break of the first pilot, ranked as the selfish optimum at its exact value, and the stopping turn in 22 of 23 games that ended by agreement.

The old economy offered almost no moments where objectives disagreed. Across positions the models actually reached, the share of trade-off moments was overall and during negotiation, the phase where all the behavioural variety lived. The mechanism is visible in the arithmetic: in of positions, the selfish optimum equals the joint optimum minus a side payment. With payments unrestricted, a selfish player picks the largest total and buys the partner's consent, so it ranks plans exactly as a joint-welfare player does. Convergence was a property of the economy, not a discovery about the models.

What changed

Two rule changes, both structural, neither touching what the models are told. Scheduled payments are capped at 12 points per deal and transfers are disabled, so consent cannot be bought without limit. Payments may only fall in the second half of the game, so a promise outstays the window in which a deal can be cancelled and becomes something that can be broken. Admission was inverted: the old rule required one clearly best plan, the new rule requires at least two near-best plans with materially different splits that need different opening moves.

Eight tuning rounds produced the current settings; two of them made things worse and are recorded as such. Every threshold was frozen before the scenarios that judge it were generated, and one candidate design failed its held-out check and its seed range was burned. The final settings were then generated fresh from untouched seeds and tested once: trade-off moments at of visited positions, uniform across phases, and trust moments in 48 scripted games.

Checking the instrument before spending

Three offline checks, each with a threshold set in advance. The measurement vocabulary buckets actions into classes, and a disagreement invisible at class resolution cannot be measured: of plan-level disagreements survive bucketing. The separability test was simulated against known differences at the collection size we planned: it detects of them, with a false-positive rate of . That number is the reason the results below are interpretable at all; the equivalent figure in the first pilot's own simulation was 25.7%, low enough that its null was uninformative about the models.

Two weaker results, reported as weak. When a prediction missed, it sat slightly closer to the predictor's own tendencies than to the target's ( bits, against for a third-model comparison, 100 cells). Replaying the injected position with and without the forecast line shows the text was read at about half the moments and ignored at the rest, so the reading is uptake without benefit rather than pure inattention. At these sample sizes neither result would survive a demanding test on its own.

What we conclude

1. The environment, not the models, produced the first pilot's null. An exact enumeration of the old economy finds objective disagreement at 0.8% of visited positions and none at all during negotiation, with the mechanism identified: unrestricted side payments make a selfish planner rank plans exactly as a joint planner does. Two rule changes remove that, and the same measurement then separates two of three model pairs. The bounded claim is about this economy; it says nothing about how often real deployments have the same property, though transferable value and free side payments are not rare.

2. Behavioural evidence about a target did not improve prediction of it, under stakes. Neither ordered history, counts, nor shuffled history beat the board alone, all within 0.02 bits of zero, against a registered threshold of 0.05. This replicates the Commons null in a consequential world and closes its main escape route, because here the targets are demonstrably distinguishable. The claim is bounded to these prompts, these four views, and behavioural records; reasoning traces were captured but never shown to predictors, which is the obvious next experiment.

3. A self-prediction advantage appeared where the first pilot hinted at one. 0.106 excess bits, p = 0.008, on identical information, registered in advance. It is an observer-frame advantage measured at negotiation positions in one economy with three models. Whether it is privileged access or a shared cause (a model recognising its own style in a position it would have produced) is not decided by this design.

4. Prediction error tracks certified choice. Error is 44% higher where enumeration proves a genuine dilemma exists. This is the cleanest thing in the study to carry into future designs: it lets an experiment aim its budget at positions where prediction is a real question.

5. Anticipation did not pay. Real forecasts of a partner produced no advantage over filler, and the conditioning check rules out simple inattention as the whole story. Ten games per arm is not enough to make this a finding; it is enough to say the effect is not large and obvious.

6. What this cost, and what it does not show. The rebuild and the whole registered campaign ran for about $370 of live model calls, with every threshold, amendment and burned seed range on the record, including one criterion redefined after it failed and re-tested on games it had never seen. Three models, one economy, 64 frozen positions. The nine-model cohort is the next test of whether any of it generalises.
